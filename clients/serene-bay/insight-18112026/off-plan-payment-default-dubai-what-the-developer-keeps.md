An off-plan payment default in Dubai does not let a developer simply cancel the sale and keep everything. The process is fixed by [Article 11 of Law No. 13 of 2008, as replaced by Law No. 19 of 2017](https://dlp.dubai.gov.ae/Legislation%20Reference/2017/Law%20No.%20(19)%20of%202017%20Amending%20Law%20No.%20(13)%20of%202008%20Regulating%20the%20Interim%20Real%20Property%20Register%20in%20the%20Emirate%20of%20Dubai.html). The developer must report the default to the Dubai Land Department (DLD), which gives the buyer 30 days to pay or settle. Only after that can the developer act, and what it may keep depends on one number: the unit's percentage of completion, as certified by the DLD. Above 60 per cent, the developer may terminate and keep up to 40 per cent of the unit's price. Below 60 per cent, once construction has started, the ceiling is 25 per cent. If work never began for reasons outside the developer's control, it may keep up to 30 per cent of what the buyer has paid. If the regulator cancels the project, every dirham comes back.

The detail that catches people is the base. Most of those caps are a share of the price, not of the money paid. A buyer who has paid less than the cap can lose all of it.

## Why the question matters more as buildings complete

Dubai is in the middle of a heavy handover cycle. Cushman & Wakefield Core expects about 55,600 homes to be delivered in 2026, which it describes as the highest annual completion volume since 2008, and a further 60,000-plus in 2027, as [reported by Khaleej Times on 20 August 2026](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/business/dubai-set-for-highest-annual-home-deliveries-since-2008-as-new-launches-slow). Those are market-wide counts. In a single building the pressure arrives on one date, when the completion instalment falls due.

In a plan that holds a large share of the price back until handover, completion is when the biggest cheque falls due, and when a buyer who planned to refinance or sell discovers whether the valuation and the buyer pool are there. It is also when the law gives the developer its widest set of options. A plan that looked comfortable at booking deserves a second reading at that point.

## The 30-day notice comes first

The statute sets a sequence that a developer cannot skip. Under [Article 11(a)](https://dlp.dubai.gov.ae/Legislation%20Reference/2017/Law%20No.%20(19)%20of%202017%20Amending%20Law%20No.%20(13)%20of%202008%20Regulating%20the%20Interim%20Real%20Property%20Register%20in%20the%20Emirate%20of%20Dubai.html):

1. The developer notifies the DLD on the DLD's prescribed form, setting out exactly which obligations the buyer has breached.
2. The DLD verifies the breach and serves a dated, written notice giving the buyer 30 days to perform. Where it can, the DLD also mediates a settlement, which is then signed as an addendum to the sale agreement.
3. If the 30 days pass without payment or settlement, the DLD issues the developer an official document confirming that the procedure was followed and stating the unit's percentage of completion, calculated under RERA's standards.

Only with that document in hand may the developer use its remedies, and it may do so without going to court or arbitration. The law also declares these rules part of public order: an act that ignores them is void. A text message from a sales team announcing that a unit has been cancelled is not the statutory route.

The 30 days are the most useful window a buyer in difficulty will have. A settlement reached then, such as a rescheduled instalment, becomes part of the contract.

## What the developer may keep after an off-plan payment default in Dubai

The remedies step down as the building's completion falls. The figures below are the statutory ceilings, the most a developer may retain, not a fixed charge.

| Unit completion (DLD certified) | What the developer may do | Most it may keep | When any excess is refunded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above 80% | Keep the contract and claim the balance; ask the DLD to sell the unit at public auction; or terminate | Up to 40% of the unit price, if it terminates | Within one year of termination, or 60 days after resale, whichever is earlier |
| 60% to 80% | Terminate | Up to 40% of the unit price | Within one year, or 60 days after resale, whichever is earlier |
| Below 60%, construction started | Terminate | Up to 25% of the unit price | Within one year, or 60 days after resale, whichever is earlier |
| Not started, for reasons beyond the developer's control | Terminate | Up to 30% of the amounts paid | Within 60 days of termination |
| Project cancelled by RERA | Must refund | Nothing | Under the escrow law, Law No. 8 of 2007 |

Source: [Law No. 19 of 2017, Article 11](https://dlp.dubai.gov.ae/Legislation%20Reference/2017/Law%20No.%20(19)%20of%202017%20Amending%20Law%20No.%20(13)%20of%202008%20Regulating%20the%20Interim%20Real%20Property%20Register%20in%20the%20Emirate%20of%20Dubai.html), Dubai Legislation Portal. The same reading appears in [Khaleej Times' account of the rules, published 10 October 2026](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/missed-off-plan-payments-dubai-developer-legally-do).

Above 80 per cent, termination is only one choice. The developer may instead hold the buyer to the contract and pursue the balance, or have the unit auctioned and charge the buyer the costs of the sale. Near completion, a defaulting buyer cannot assume the outcome is a partial refund.

## Price or paid: the base changes the answer

The 40 and 25 per cent ceilings are measured against the value of the unit in the sale agreement. The 30 per cent ceiling, for a project that never started, is measured against what the buyer has paid. That difference decides how much comes back.

Take a unit priced at AED 2,000,000. The figures below are our calculation, applying the statutory ceilings to two buyers: one who has paid 50 per cent (AED 1,000,000) and one who has paid 30 per cent (AED 600,000).

| Unit completion | Most the developer may keep | Buyer who paid AED 1,000,000 gets back at least | Buyer who paid AED 600,000 gets back at least |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above 80% (if it terminates) | AED 800,000 | AED 200,000 | Nothing |
| 60% to 80% | AED 800,000 | AED 200,000 | Nothing |
| Below 60%, started | AED 500,000 | AED 500,000 | AED 100,000 |
| Not started | 30% of amounts paid | AED 700,000 | AED 420,000 |

![Stacked bars for an AED 2,000,000 unit with AED 1,000,000 paid: the developer may keep up to AED 800,000 above 60 per cent completion, AED 500,000 below 60 per cent, and AED 300,000 if work never started](/images/off-plan-payment-default-dubai-what-the-developer-keeps-chart-01.webp)

The second buyer is the uncomfortable case. With 30 per cent paid and the tower past 60 per cent complete, the ceiling of AED 800,000 is larger than everything paid in, so the statute leaves no excess to refund. Payment plans that keep most of the price back until handover are attractive at booking for exactly the reason that makes them exposed at completion: the buyer's stake is smaller than the developer's cap.

These are ceilings. A developer may keep less, and the DLD's mediation step exists to reach a negotiated outcome. But a buyer should plan on the statutory figure, not on goodwill.

## How a buyer can see where a project stands

The band a default falls into turns on the completion percentage, so it is worth knowing that figure before it matters. The DLD's free [project status enquiry](https://dubailand.gov.ae/en/eservices/real-estate-project-status-landing/real-estate-project-status/) shows a project's progress by name or number, and the same service sits in the Dubai REST app. Checked at booking and again before each large instalment, it shows which band the buyer would be in if a payment slipped.

Three things are worth reading in the sale agreement alongside it:

- **The schedule itself.** Which instalments are tied to dates and which to construction milestones, and how large the completion payment is.
- **Late-payment terms.** Penalties and interest for a late instalment are contractual and sit apart from the Article 11 process.
- **Exit routes.** Whether the developer permits assignment before handover, and at what point. Selling the contract on is usually a better outcome than default, and it is covered in [what an assignment involves](/insights/selling-off-plan-before-handover).

## Where the buyer keeps leverage

The law gives the buyer two protections that matter in practice. The first is the 30-day notice and the DLD's mediation, which exist precisely to produce a settlement. The second is Article 11(f): if the developer abuses its powers, the buyer may still go to court or to arbitration.

Abu Dhabi runs a parallel system with different bands and a longer notice period, set out in our note on [Abu Dhabi off-plan escrow](/insights/abu-dhabi-off-plan-escrow-20-per-cent-rule). The Dubai figures above should not be applied there.

The most effective protection is earlier. A payment plan should be judged against the buyer's own cash flow at the point of the largest instalment, under a less comfortable valuation than the one at booking. That is a question best asked before a reservation, by someone with no commission riding on the answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens if I miss an off-plan payment in Dubai?

The developer must first notify the Dubai Land Department, which serves a written notice giving you 30 days to pay or reach a settlement, under [Article 11 of Law No. 13 of 2008 as amended](https://dlp.dubai.gov.ae/Legislation%20Reference/2017/Law%20No.%20(19)%20of%202017%20Amending%20Law%20No.%20(13)%20of%202008%20Regulating%20the%20Interim%20Real%20Property%20Register%20in%20the%20Emirate%20of%20Dubai.html). Contractual late-payment penalties may also apply. Termination is possible only after the 30 days expire and the DLD certifies the unit's completion percentage.

### Can a Dubai developer cancel my off-plan purchase without going to court?

Yes, once the statutory steps are complete. After the 30-day notice lapses, the DLD issues the developer a document confirming the procedure and the completion percentage, and the developer may then terminate without court or arbitration. If it skips those steps, the law treats the act as void, and you may challenge any abuse of power in court or arbitration under Article 11(f).

### How much can a developer keep after an off-plan payment default in Dubai?

It depends on completion. From 60 per cent upwards, up to 40 per cent of the unit price. Below 60 per cent, once construction has started, up to 25 per cent of the price. If work never started for reasons beyond the developer's control, up to 30 per cent of the amounts paid. These are ceilings set by [Law No. 19 of 2017](https://dlp.dubai.gov.ae/Legislation%20Reference/2017/Law%20No.%20(19)%20of%202017%20Amending%20Law%20No.%20(13)%20of%202008%20Regulating%20the%20Interim%20Real%20Property%20Register%20in%20the%20Emirate%20of%20Dubai.html).

### When do I get my refund after a termination?

For a project that had started, any amount above the retained share is due within one year of termination, or within 60 days of the unit being resold, whichever comes first. Where work never started, the excess is due within 60 days of termination. If RERA cancels the project, all payments are refunded under the escrow law, Law No. 8 of 2007.

### Do the rules apply to sale agreements signed before 2017?

Yes. Article 11(d) applies the rules to every off-plan sale agreement, whether concluded before or after the 2017 law took effect, according to the [text published on the Dubai Legislation Portal](https://dlp.dubai.gov.ae/Legislation%20Reference/2017/Law%20No.%20(19)%20of%202017%20Amending%20Law%20No.%20(13)%20of%202008%20Regulating%20the%20Interim%20Real%20Property%20Register%20in%20the%20Emirate%20of%20Dubai.html). They do not cover land sale agreements that involve no off-plan sale; those follow their own contract terms.

### Is selling the unit better than defaulting?

Usually, where the developer allows an assignment. A sale passes the contract and its remaining instalments to a new buyer, and what you recover is set by the price you agree, less fees, rather than by a statutory deduction of up to 40 per cent of the unit price. It depends on the developer's assignment terms and on there being a buyer at a workable price before the next instalment falls due.
